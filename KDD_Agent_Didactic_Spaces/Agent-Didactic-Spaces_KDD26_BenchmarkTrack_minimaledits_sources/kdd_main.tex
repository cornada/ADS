\begin{abstract}
Educational pathway decisions must balance learner goals, institutional mission, and labor-market relevance, yet evaluation setups rarely expose these trade-offs.
We release a provenance-traced benchmark for multi-stakeholder pathway evaluation built from \NumCourses public university courses, \NumPrograms program descriptors, \NumOccupations O*NET occupations, and a \NumMissionsExpanded-text mission corpus, together with \NumAttributeRecords structured attribute/link records (\NumProcessedRows processed rows).
The benchmark defines standardized objectives, baselines, and robustness audits, including mission-corpus expansion and market drift across historical O*NET releases (2022--2025).
As a reference benchmarking tool, Agent-Didactic Spaces (\ADS) with Multi-Agent Pareto Evaluation (\MAPE) computes stakeholder-specific scores with auditable lenses, filters options using a tunable dominance threshold~$\tau$, and returns feasible Pareto sets with retrieval-grounded explanations.
At $\tau{=}0.25$, \MAPE yields \MainParetoSize feasible Pareto options, while scalarization baselines recover only~{\BaselineOptima}.
In a practitioner study ($n{=}13$), educators rated trade-off transparency at {4.85/5} and usefulness at {4.38/5}.
We release data and code at \BenchmarkURL and \CodeURL (O*NET content used under CC BY 4.0; see~\cite{onet_license_db}).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Educational planning systems   from course recommenders to career-alignment tools   now sit in the loop of curriculum decisions. They guide learners toward \emph{what to learn next} and, indirectly, shape which modules educators choose to create. Yet these choices rarely optimize a single objective. Learners, institutions, labor markets, and governance bodies each bring legitimate   and sometimes conflicting   criteria for what counts as a good pathway.

Despite this, most deployed pipelines still return a single ranked list. They collapse stakeholder objectives into one utility proxy and optimize it. The convenience is obvious; the cost is easier to miss. Two options with similar overall scores can encode very different balances between learner preference, mission alignment, and labor-market relevance. Once these balances are buried, governance becomes brittle: stakeholders cannot audit \emph{which} objectives were prioritized, and learners cannot reason about the opportunity cost of privileging one objective over another.

This leads to a simple question: what would pathway evaluation look like if it refused to scalarize? Early design discussions reframed the governance problem as a shift from scalar grades to a \emph{vector} notion of educational fit: evaluate each option by its alignment with multiple stakeholder ``directions'' (learner, institution, labor market, governance, and mission/values), and surface the resulting trade-offs instead of burying them in a single score.

\ADS/\MAPE is a practical operationalization   and a generalization   of this idea. \ADS makes each stakeholder ``vector'' an auditable object: a target corpus (summarized by a centroid) together with an explicit lens over the embedding space. \MAPE keeps the full vector of stakeholder similarities and surfaces trade-offs by returning a feasible Pareto set rather than a single ranking. The dominance threshold $\tau$ becomes the governance knob implied by the original sketch: tightening $\tau$ filters options that drift too far toward any single non-learner stakeholder, whereas relaxing it permits more aggressive alignment when policy allows. Our experiments instantiate four stakeholders for reproducibility; the fifth ``state/regulator'' stakeholder is represented as another \ADS agent by supplying an additional corpus and lens at deployment time.

We therefore argue that pathway evaluation should preserve stakeholder plurality and make trade-offs inspectable. We introduce \emph{Agent-Didactic Spaces} (\ADS), a representation layer that embeds educational artifacts (courses, program texts, job-role descriptions, learner statements) into a shared space. We operationalize each stakeholder as an \emph{agent}   not an autonomous RL entity, but a transparent evaluation perspective defined by (i) a target corpus, (ii) a simple auditable \emph{lens} over the embedding space, and (iii) optional normative constraints. Building on \ADS, \MAPE computes stakeholder-specific similarity scores, enforces a tunable \emph{stakeholder-dominance} feasibility constraint, and returns a \emph{feasible Pareto set} with retrieval-grounded explanations and lightweight recourse cues. The point is contestation and learner-centred choice, not silent optimization.

\paragraph{What this paper is not.}
We do \emph{not} present an LLM tutor, and we do not claim causal improvements in learning outcomes. We focus instead on an \emph{evaluation and governance} layer for pathway options using public artifacts and explicit objectives.

\paragraph{Contribution.} This paper contributes an open benchmark and a reference benchmarking tool for multi-stakeholder educational pathway evaluation:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textit{Open, provenance-traced benchmark.} We release a benchmark built from \NumCourses public university courses, \NumPrograms program descriptors, \NumOccupations O*NET occupations, and a \NumMissionsExpanded-text mission corpus, with \NumAttributeRecords structured attribute/link records (\NumProcessedRows processed rows).

\section{Related Work and Positioning}
\label{sec:related}
\ADS/\MAPE sits at the intersection of four research threads: (i) educational recommenders and pathway planning, (ii) mapping education artifacts to labor-market needs, (iii) multi-objective optimization with normative constraints, and (iv) multi-stakeholder evaluation and contestable AI. We briefly review each line and highlight the gap our framework targets: making stakeholder trade-offs explicit and governable rather than implicitly scalarized.

\subsection{Educational pathway recommendation and course representations}
Educational recommender systems recommend learning resources, courses, or sequences using learner profiles and structured constraints such as prerequisites. Recent surveys emphasize the diversity of objectives and evaluation practices in this space, and note that many deployed systems still compress evaluation into a scalar proxy.\cite{dasilva2022slrERS,aucancela2023ersslr}
Learning-path recommendation often relies on structured knowledge representations such as knowledge graphs.\cite{shi2020}
Course recommendation also appears in mainstream AI venues: for example, IJCAI work explores graph- and reinforcement-learning based formulations for course recommendation and educational resource feedback.\cite{hhcor2024,dfrp2024}
In parallel, representation-learning approaches such as Course2Vec-style models learn course embeddings from enrolment data to support similarity, prediction, and recommendation.\cite{morsy2019course2vec,jiang2020multifactorCourse2vec,xu2023courseEmbeddings}
\paragraph{How ADS differs:} rather than producing a single ranked list, \ADS makes stakeholder perspectives explicit and returns a \emph{set} of feasible Pareto options to surface trade-offs.
Complementary work uses LLM-based educational assistants for tutoring, feedback, and assessment.\cite{coderagent2025,collabAgents2025,llmGrading2025,handByHand2025,livepoem2025} \ADS is orthogonal: it targets multi-stakeholder evaluation and governance of candidate options, before (or alongside) downstream tutoring or delivery.

\subsection{Skills and labor-market alignment}
Mapping educational content to labor-market needs underpins career-oriented advising and job recommendation. Prior work embeds job offers, skills, and candidates to support matching and recommendation, including graph-based formulations and embedding-based methods~\cite{giabelli2021skills2job,kara2022skillEmbeddings,leon2024transversalSkills}.

\paragraph{How ADS differs:} we treat labor-market alignment as \emph{one stakeholder objective} among others (e.g., mission and learner), not as the sole target to optimize.

\subsection{Multi-objective optimization and normative constraints}
Pareto optimality and scalarization methods are standard tools for preserving trade-offs in multi-objective optimization.\cite{roijers2013survey_mosdm,deb2002nsga2}
Recent IJCAI work studies multi-objective learning via scalarization (e.g., random scalarization neural bandits) and normative behaviour constraints in decision-making (e.g., ``restraining bolts'').\cite{monb2025,restrainingBolts2025}
Multi-agent and multi-objective perspectives also appear in policy settings where equity-related trade-offs matter.\cite{equityMAMORL2025}
\paragraph{How ADS differs:} we use these tools as an \emph{evaluation layer} for educational pathway options, and we integrate a simple, auditable \emph{feasibility constraint} that limits stakeholder dominance.

\subsection{Multi-stakeholder evaluation and contestable AI}
Multi-stakeholder recommender evaluation argues that recommender quality cannot be assessed from a single user's perspective alone.\cite{abdollahpouri2020multistakeholder,burke2025mseval,ge2024trustworthyRS}
Contestable AI frameworks push this further: affected parties should be able to challenge and revise algorithmic decisions, shifting emphasis from prediction accuracy to governance and participation.\cite{aler2020contestable,alfrink2023contestable,dignum2025contesting}
Explainable recommendation surveys motivate transparency and user understanding in recommendation pipelines.\cite{zhang2020explainableRS}
Counterfactual explanations and actionable recourse offer concrete ``what-if'' tools for challenge and revision.\cite{wachter2017counterfactual,ustun2019recourse,robustx2025}
Fairness constraints and stability in ranking/decision pipelines matter when pathway recommendations affect access and opportunity.\cite{ekstrand2019fairness,dwork2012fairness,hardt2016equality,fairRankAgg2025,fairdas2024}
\paragraph{How ADS differs:} \ADS/\MAPE makes stakeholder objectives explicit look-up objects (targets + lenses), returns auditable trade-offs as feasible Pareto sets, and attaches explanation/recourse objects to each option to support contestation rather than opaque optimization.

\section{Problem Setting}
\label{sec:setting}
\paragraph{Options and pathways.}
Let $\mathcal{X}$ be a set of educational options (courses, modules, projects). A \emph{pathway} is a sequence $\pi=(x_1,\dots,x_T)$ subject to feasibility constraints (e.g., prerequisites), denoted $\pi\in\Pi\subseteq\mathcal{X}^T$. In this paper's experiments we focus on \emph{option-level} evaluation (length-$1$ pathways), and treat multi-step planning as an extension (Appendix in Supplement).

\paragraph{Stakeholder agents.}
We consider a finite set of stakeholders $\mathcal{A}$ (e.g., learner, institution, labor market, regulator/mission). In \ADS, an ``agent'' is not a learning RL entity; it is a stakeholder perspective parameterized by a lens and a target corpus.

\paragraph{Goal.}
Given $\mathcal{X}$ (or $\Pi$), the system returns (i) stakeholder-specific scores, (ii) the feasible Pareto set, and (iii) stakeholder-typed explanations that support learner-centred choice and contestation.

\section{Agent-Didactic Spaces and \MAPE}
\label{sec:method}
\subsection{Shared representation space}
Let $\mathcal{T}$ denote text artifacts: course descriptions/syllabi, job-role descriptions, mission statements, learner statements, etc. A representation map  $r:\mathcal{T}\to\mathbb{R}^d$ embeds each artifact into a shared space. For an option $x\in\mathcal{X}$ with artifacts $\mathcal{T}_x$, we form an option profile by mean-pooling and L2-normalization:
\begin{equation}
  v_x = \frac{\bar{v}_x}{\|\bar{v}_x\|}, \quad \text{where } \bar{v}_x = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_x|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_x} r(t).
\end{equation}
Similarly, each stakeholder $a$ has a \emph{target corpus} $\mathcal{T}_a$ and a target profile (centroid)
\begin{equation}
  \mu_a = \frac{\bar{\mu}_a}{\|\bar{\mu}_a\|_2}, \quad \text{where } \bar{\mu}_a = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_a|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_a} r(t).
\end{equation}

\subsection{Stakeholder lenses}
Each stakeholder $a$ is associated with a lens $L_a$ that reweights or transforms the embedding space, $L_a:\mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^d.$
We focus on auditable linear lenses used in the prototype:
(i) \emph{identity} $L_a(v)=v$;
(ii) \emph{diagonal} $L_a(v)=\mathrm{diag}(\ell_a)\,v$ with nonnegative weights $\ell_a$; and
(iii) \emph{learned diagonal} lenses trained from labeled corpora via one-vs-rest logistic regression on embedding dimensions (details in Supplement).

\subsection{Multi-objective similarity scores}
\label{subsec:scores}
For each stakeholder $a$, we define a raw similarity score as cosine similarity after applying the lens:
\begin{equation}
  \tilde{s}_a(x) = \frac{L_a(v_x) \cdot L_a(\mu_a)}{\|L_a(v_x)\| \|L_a(\mu_a)\|}.
\end{equation}
\paragraph{Cross-stakeholder calibration.}
Raw cosine similarities are not directly comparable across stakeholders due to differences in target corpus geometry (e.g., mission texts may cluster more tightly than job-role descriptions). We therefore calibrate scores by z-score normalization within each stakeholder:
\begin{equation}
  s_a(x) = \frac{\tilde{s}_a(x) - \hat{\mu}_a}{\hat{\sigma}_a},
\end{equation}
where $\hat{\mu}_a = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{X}|}\sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}} \tilde{s}_a(x)$ and $\hat{\sigma}_a$ are the empirical mean and standard deviation of raw scores across all options. This ensures that a score of $s_a(x) = 1$ means ``one standard deviation above the mean alignment for stakeholder $a$.''
Collecting calibrated scores yields a vector-valued evaluation
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{s}(x) = (s_{a_1}(x),\dots,s_{a_m}(x)).
\end{equation}

For pathways $\pi$, we can aggregate option profiles (e.g., mean pooling) or aggregate per-step scores; in experiments we evaluate options ($T=1$).

\subsection{Normative feasibility: stakeholder-dominance risk (autonomy drift)}
\label{subsec:constraint}
Pareto sets preserve trade-offs but do not encode normative constraints. We therefore define a feasibility filter that flags options where \emph{any} stakeholder objective dominates others beyond a threshold $\tau$.
We define a symmetric dominance-gap score as the maximum pairwise gap:
\begin{equation}
  g(x) = \max_{a,b \in \mathcal{A}, a \neq b} \left( s_a(x) - s_b(x) \right),
\end{equation}
and call an option feasible if $g(x) \le \tau$. For governance scenarios where a specific stakeholder $a^\star$ (e.g., market) requires additional scrutiny, we define a directed variant:
\begin{equation}
  g_{a^\star}(x) = s_{a^\star}(x) - \max_{b\in\mathcal{A}\setminus\{a^\star\}} s_b(x).
\end{equation}
In our main experiments we report both: the symmetric $g(x)$ ensures no stakeholder dominates, while $g_{\text{market}}(x)$ addresses the specific governance concern of market capture. An option is feasible under the combined constraint if $\max(g(x), g_{\text{market}}(x)) \le \tau$.
Intuitively, tightening $\tau$ prevents single-objective recommendations from overwhelming other stakeholder interests.

\subsection{Feasible Pareto set}
Let $\mathcal{X}_\tau=\{x\in\mathcal{X}: g(x)\le\tau\}$ denote feasible options. For vectors $\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^m$, we say $\mathbf{u}$ \emph{dominates} $\mathbf{v}$ (write $\mathbf{u}\succ \mathbf{v}$) if $u_k\ge v_k$ for all $k$ and $u_k>v_k$ for some $k$. The feasible Pareto set is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{P}_\tau = \big\{x\in\mathcal{X}_\tau : \nexists y\in\mathcal{X}_\tau \text{ s.t. } \mathbf{s}(y)\succ \mathbf{s}(x)\big\}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Explanations and recourse}
\label{subsec:explain}
For each $x\in\mathcal{P}_\tau$, \MAPE returns a structured explanation object: stakeholder scores $\mathbf{s}(x)$; the constraint gap $g(x)$; and, for each stakeholder, the nearest target artifacts (top-$k$) under $\cos(L_a(\cdot),L_a(\mu_a))$. This grounds qualitative explanations in retrieved text rather than free-form generation. In addition, we provide lightweight recourse cues by reporting which target artifacts increase a chosen objective the most (e.g., ``which market roles you are closest to'') and by computing score deltas under counterfactual edits (Supplement).

\subsection{Robustness and stability evaluation}
Because embedding-based scores are sensitive to representation and target choices, we evaluate stability by perturbing embeddings and/or target corpora and recomputing $\mathcal{P}_\tau$. We report set-level overlap (e.g., Jaccard similarity) and within-set rank stability (e.g., Spearman correlation) as a function of perturbation magnitude.

\subsection{Algorithm sketch and implementation mapping}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:mape} sketches the end-to-end pipeline.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\MAPE in \ADS (prototype pipeline)}
\label{alg:mape}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Option artifacts $\{\mathcal{T}_x\}_{x\in\mathcal{X}}$; stakeholder target corpora $\{\mathcal{T}_a\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}$; lens specs; dominance threshold $\tau$
\STATE Embed all artifacts via $r$; aggregate to option profiles $v_x$ and target profiles $\mu_a$
\STATE Construct stakeholder lenses $L_a$ (identity/diagonal/learned)
\FOR{each option $x\in\mathcal{X}$}
  \STATE Compute stakeholder scores $s_a(x)=\cos(L_a(v_x),L_a(\mu_a))$
  \STATE Compute dominance gap $g(x)$ and feasibility flag $\mathbb{I}[g(x)\le\tau]$
\ENDFOR
\STATE Compute feasible Pareto set $\mathcal{P}_\tau$ from $\{\mathbf{s}(x): x\in\mathcal{X}_\tau\}$
\STATE For each $x\in\mathcal{P}_\tau$, attach explanation + recourse objects (nearest artifacts, score deltas)
\STATE Optionally: perturb embeddings/targets and recompute stability metrics
\RETURN $\mathcal{P}_\tau$ with explanations and stability report
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Stakeholder Engagement and Practitioner Validation}
\label{sec:collab}

We designed \ADS/\MAPE in conversation with practice. A design-phase partnership with an \IndustryPartner surfaced a recurring governance failure mode: curriculum and pathway choices must balance market relevance with pedagogical integrity, yet teams often negotiate this balance implicitly. Without an explicit representation of trade-offs, it is difficult to justify decisions, document them for accountability, or contest them when stakeholders disagree.

\subsection{Formative stakeholder interviews (partner organization)}
We conducted semi-structured formative interviews with three senior stakeholders (product leadership, academic affairs leadership, senior instructional designer) to elicit requirements and stress-test early \ADS/\MAPE prototypes (protocol and additional anonymized quotes in Supplementary Material). A lightweight thematic synthesis converged on three recurring needs: (i) make market - pedagogy tensions explicit rather than letting ``whoever has the louder voice that day'' decide; (ii) prefer a Pareto set over a single ranked list, framing the frontier as a \emph{decision map}; and (iii) treat the dominance threshold $\tau$ as a configurable governance guardrail against long-run drift toward purely market-driven design.

\subsection{Practitioner validation study}
We then asked whether these design choices remain useful for day-to-day practitioners beyond the partner organization. We ran a remote study with 13 experienced educational professionals drawn from a diverse range of institutions (see Supplementary Material for demographics). Participants examined a Pareto frontier visualization, completed a think-aloud protocol, and then filled out a quantitative survey on a 5-point Likert scale.

Figure~\ref{fig:verification} summarizes the main quantitative findings. Participants strongly endorsed the framework's central promise: they rated {Trade-off Transparency} at {4.85/5}, indicating that the frontier makes conflicts in curriculum design visible rather than implicit. They also rated {Usefulness (vs. ranked lists)} at {4.38/5}, suggesting that practitioners prefer Pareto trade-off presentation to a single scalarized ranking.

Ratings for {Clarity} (4.23/5), {Confidence} (3.69/5), and {Actionability} (3.62/5) met the support threshold (means $\ge 3.5$), but Confidence and Actionability remained moderate. In practice, participants wanted stronger ``drill-down'' into the evidence behind individual Pareto points and more interactive support for turning a point into concrete design actions. {Cognitive Load} (3.38/5) did not reach the support threshold. We therefore do not claim that \ADS/\MAPE reduces cognitive effort; instead, it can increase \emph{productive cognitive engagement} by shifting effort from searching to interpreting trade-offs.

Qualitative feedback reinforces this interpretation. Participants described the frontier as a mechanism for reflective governance (P02: \textit{"This is making me realize I've been making these trade-offs unconsciously."}) and accountability (P07: \textit{"If the dean asks why we added this module, I can point to the trade-off analysis."}). Several also linked the approach to reducing costly trial-and-error in curriculum design (P13: \textit{"We discovered it through expensive trial and error. This visualization would have helped us see that earlier."}). One participant (P06) was notably more skeptical, cautioning against reifying embedding similarity as ground truth. We treat this as a boundary condition: \ADS/\MAPE is a governance scaffold for structuring discussion, not a substitute for expert judgement (Supplementary).

\subsection{Implications and next steps}
Taken together, the formative interviews and practitioner validation support positioning \ADS/\MAPE as an evaluation and governance layer: it externalizes stakeholder trade-offs and makes the choice of guardrails explicit. The next step is to translate transparency into action. Concretely, we plan interactive drill-down into individual Pareto points (retrieved evidence, uncertainty cues, and ``why this point'' explanations), richer control knobs for turning frontier navigation into concrete curriculum edits, integration of operational constraints (time/cost/capacity), and controlled comparisons against ranked-list baselines in realistic course-design tasks. Measuring downstream learner outcomes (retention, completion, job placement) requires longitudinal deployment and remains future work.



\section{Experiments}

\label{sec:experiments}

The practitioner pilot in Sec.~\ref{sec:collab} tests whether educators find Pareto trade-off presentation useful. The offline experiments below ask a different question: when we apply \ADS/\MAPE to public educational and labor-market artifacts at scale, does it expose a non-trivial, stable trade-off surface or does scalarization already capture everything that matters? We answer by measuring (i) how much of the frontier scalar baselines recover, (ii) how the governance knob $\tau$ reshapes feasibility and Pareto size, (iii) robustness across encoders and perturbations, and (iv) runtime scaling.

\subsection{Dataset, objectives, and provenance}
\label{subsec:data}
We evaluate \ADS/\MAPE on an open, provenance-traced benchmark built from {public university course artifacts} and {public labor-market taxonomies}. The released benchmark contains {\NumPrimaryArtifacts} primary text artifacts (\NumCourses courses, \NumPrograms program descriptors, \NumOccupations O*NET occupations, and a \NumMissionsExpanded-text mission corpus) and {\NumAttributeRecords} structured attribute/link records (O*NET skills/tasks/technologies, CIP - SOC crosswalk links, and course$\leftrightarrow$occupation links), totalling {\NumProcessedRows} processed rows across all released tables (Table~\ref{tab:dataset_overview}).

\paragraph{Access and licensing.}

\subsection{Experimental setup}
We embed artifacts with sentence encoders from \texttt{sentence-transformers}: MiniLM (\texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2}, 384d) as the primary encoder and MPNet (\texttt{all-mpnet-base-v2}, 768d) as an ablation. A stub (random) encoder provides a sanity baseline. For each option or target corpus, we mean-pool artifact embeddings and L2-normalize before cosine scoring. Unless stated otherwise, we use identity lenses and the default dominance threshold $\tau{=}0.25$.

\subsection{Baselines and metrics}
We compare \MAPE to scalarization baselines: weighted-sum maximization over a weight grid, random scalarization (sampling weight vectors), and lexicographic orderings. We report: feasible ratio $|\mathcal{X}_\tau|/|\mathcal{X}|$, feasible Pareto size $|\mathcal{P}_\tau|$, set overlap (Jaccard) under perturbations and across seeds, and runtime scaling (with cached embeddings).

\paragraph{Why we do not compare to SOTA course recommenders.}
We do not directly compare \MAPE to state-of-the-art course recommenders (e.g., Course2Vec-style models\cite{morsy2019course2vec}) because our framework operates at a different layer of abstraction. \ADS/\MAPE is an evaluation and governance tool for surfacing stakeholder trade-offs among a \emph{given} set of options, not a recommender system for predicting user preferences or generating candidate pathways. Future work could explore integrating \MAPE as a re-ranking or filtering stage on top of a traditional recommender.

\paragraph{Robustness, transfer, and efficiency.}
Under score perturbations, full Pareto membership can be sensitive (e.g., Jaccard $\approx 0.07$ at $\varepsilon{=}0.02$), but a robust core emerges in ADS-2D: a target bootstrap preserves {9/11} Pareto options (82\%) (Supplementary Table~S7). Cross-university evaluation in ADS-2D yields disjoint Pareto sets for UC Berkeley vs. MIT (Jaccard $=0$), suggesting that institutional corpora contribute genuinely distinct options (Supplementary Table~S5). {Market time travel} across four historical O*NET releases (2022 - 2025) shows a small but measurable drift in stakeholder targets, with {\MarketDriftTurnover} Pareto turnover (Jaccard \MarketDriftJaccard), validating \ADS's design for \emph{updatable target corpora} (see Supplementary). \section{Limitations, Ethics, and Reproducibility}
\label{sec:ethics}
\paragraph{Limitations.}
\ADS inherits limitations of embedding-based representations: scores depend on corpus coverage, suffer under domain shift, and can reflect representational bias. More fundamentally, lenses and target corpora encode value judgements. If we mis-specify them, the resulting trade-off surface can be misleading even if the optimization is correct. In this paper we evaluate option-level pathways; multi-step planning adds prerequisite constraints and compounds error across steps. Our pilot results also indicate that the framework may not reduce cognitive load; instead, it can increase \emph{productive cognitive engagement} by making trade-offs explicit and requiring interpretation.

We report a practitioner validation study ($n{=}13$) supporting trade-off transparency and perceived usefulness, but this is not evidence of improved learner outcomes. Establishing causal impact (e.g., retention, completion, downstream employment) requires longitudinal deployment and remains future work.

\paragraph{Ethical considerations.}
We use public/open artifacts and avoid PII. A key risk is over-optimizing for market alignment at the expense of learner autonomy or mission commitments. \MAPE addresses this by (i) making objectives explicit and inspectable, (ii) exposing trade-offs via Pareto sets, and (iii) enforcing a tunable dominance constraint. Nevertheless, any deployment should include human oversight and mechanisms for contestation.

\paragraph{Failure modes and governance.}
Important failure modes include: (i) {embedding bias} or domain shift that changes similarity scores; (ii) {target mis-specification} (e.g., incomplete or biased market/mission corpora); (iii) {objective gaming} where users optimize for a visible objective at the expense of latent goals; and (iv) {stakeholder capture} where a single objective is treated as the ``true'' goal. Because target corpora are explicit inputs, they are also a potential misuse surface: a malicious or careless actor could manipulate outputs by substituting, poisoning, or cherry-picking the target corpora. \ADS/\MAPE mitigates these risks by making objectives and targets explicit (auditable corpora + lenses), returning a \emph{set} of trade-off options rather than a single ranking, and logging provenance so that disputed outputs can be traced to a concrete dataset snapshot. We treat the dominance threshold $\tau$ as a governance parameter selected with stakeholder input, not as a purely technical hyperparameter.

\section{Conclusion}
We introduced \ADS, a representation layer for multi-stakeholder educational pathway evaluation, and \MAPE, a feasible Pareto evaluation engine with an explicit dominance guardrail, retrieval-grounded explanations, and robustness tests. The core idea is simple: instead of hiding value judgements inside a single score, we expose stakeholder plurality and make the trade-offs contestable.
